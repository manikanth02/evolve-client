FROM LONELINESS TO ENDURING LOVE: THE PATH OF SURRENDER
Here's an unsettling but necessary truth: when a person constantly seeks a partner to fix their loneliness, they're already in danger, not just emotionally but existentially. That kind of helplessness sends a signal out into the world—and the sharks of lust, addiction, and codependency circle in. They feed on that kind of ache.
But loneliness is not necessarily a sign that one needs another person. More often, it is a deeper call — a call to know oneself, spiritual nature, and ultimately, our eternal relationship with the Supreme Lord.
The Bhagavad-gita, though set on a battlefield, speaks not on a battlefield, speaks not
just to warriors, but to all who stand at the crossroads of confusion and longing. It does not urge the seeker to find a soulmate, but to find the soul's true beloved: the all-attractive Lord, Śrī Kṛṣṇa.
Rather than chasing love in the external world, Kṛṣṇa urges the cultivation of refined spiritual intelligence that sees through illusion and turns inward. He directs the heart toward sva-dharma — to engage in devotion wherein He reveals a higher vision of duty, selflessness, and divine connection. And at the heart of that vision is śaraṇāgati — seeking devotional shelter of the Lord.
Because without surrender, the alternatives — ego, desire, control — bind us. We keep chasing love, power, recognition, security... and still feel empty. Kṛṣṇa calls for surrender not to take anything from us, but to give us everything that's real. He's not a taker; He's the supreme reciprocator. When we surrender, it means we stop placing ourselves at the center and let the actual center — God — take His rightful place. That shift changes everything.
Śrīla Bhaktivinoda Thakur writes: Ātma-nivedana, tuwā pade kori, eho māna-sukha pāi: "I offer my soul unto Your lotus feet, and in that surrender, I find real happiness." To him, surrender is not the end of desire but its purification. It transforms love from a need-based emotion into a divine offering. Instead of seeking love to fill a void, the surrendered soul becomes an instrument of love itself.
The śaraṇāgati path teaches us to stop chasing love in the world and start anchoring love in divine service. And once we learn that, our human relationships begin reflecting it — less grasping, more giving; less judging, more understanding; less hurting, more healing. Such love, rooted in surrender and free from self-centered desire, is not a distant ideal — it's a lived reality in the bhakti tradition.
BE THE VOICE THAT SPARKS TRUE LOVE
A shining example is the story of Thirumangai Ālvār, one of the twelve revered poet-saints of South India. Born as Neelan, a proud and powerful chieftain under Chola royalty, he was renowned for his valor and ambition. But everything changed when he encountered Kumudavalli, a humble and devout Vaiṣṇavi.
Captivated by her simplicity, he proposed to marry her. But as an independent woman of respect in society and with her heart centered on Lord Nārāyaṇa, she agreed to marry him only under one condition: not wealth, not prestige, not attention but devotional service. He must feed 1,000 Vaiṣṇavas every day for an extended period as an act of devotion to Lord Nārāyaṇa.
Neelan, inspired by love, cultivated the desire to serve the Vaiṣṇavas, agreed and began this massive charity. As time went on, his wealth couldn't sustain it. That's the power of service. Neelan for the first time found helpless to serve the devotees of the Lord.
During one such desperate days where he had exhausted his resources, he tried to exact valuables from a group of travelers. But it was in fact, Lord Nārāyaṇa and His consort disguised as travelers to test Neelan. As he tried to draw their ornaments, he couldn't remove the anklet from the lotus feet of the Lord. Perplexed, he tried all his means, but to no avail. That's when the Lordships revealed their true form and granted Neelan divine wisdom and grace — transforming him into Thirumangai Ālvār.
What began as a romantic pursuit became a spiritual awakening. Neelan's love evolved from possession to sacrifice, from attraction to realization. Through devotional service, his heart softened and, in time he was blessed with divine grace — becoming Thirumangai Ālvār, a saint whose poetry and surrender continue to inspire seekers even today.
Kumudavalli wasn't just a woman he loved—she was the light that redirected his passion toward divine purpose. Her spiritual conviction awakened within Neelan the dormant service spirit towards the Lord.
DON'T LOOK FOR COMPLETION—LOOK FOR CONNECTION
Before connecting with another soul, the Vaiṣṇava...
ācāryas tell us to connect with the Supreme Soul first, because only a heart full of devotion has enough strength to fully love another human being unconditionally. In a world where relationships often revolve around need, chemistry, or comfort, Kumudavalli's example reminds us that the highest love calls us to become more, not just feel more.
Kumudavalli was that voice for Thirumangai Ālvār. Who are we becoming in the name of love? And are we, like her, courageous enough to motivate our loved ones to come closer to the divine? Or are we critical and mean or perhaps too secular to connect our loved ones with the Lord?
Here's the brutal reality: if we enter a relationship hoping it will heal our trauma, we're setting ourself and our partner up for failure. Romance doesn't resolve our existential crisis. If anything, it magnifies it. That's the principle: marriage is a magnifier. If we're anxious, insecure, addicted, those issues will not magically dissolve in marriage. They will metastasize.
Love that doesn't elevate will eventually exhaust. Kumudavalli's life shows that the most fulfilling relationships are not about completing each other. They're about connecting both hearts to something higher—to the Lord, the perennial fountainhead of all that is love.
And this isn't just abstract theology—it's practical psychology. We project our unmet emotional needs onto our partners. We expect them to be gods. There's an old story about a woman who said, "Before we got married, he called me a goddess. After we got married, he became an atheist." That's what happens when we confuse romance for fulfillment.
LOVE IS NOT DEPENDENCY!
It won't be an overreach to say that many men look for a wife who can mother them, and women look for a husband who can father them. At its core, this reflects an unspoken expectation: the desire for a partner to nurture, heal, and protect. But here's the truth: no human being, no matter how loving or capable, can truly fill those deep emotional or spiritual voids.
When we meet our Creator first, our heart is filled, and we no longer expect our mate to serve our emotional vacuum. We don't seek validation or solace from them. Instead, we learn to serve them with grace, with the heart of a partner who is already full of love. We can give them our best without expecting anything in return, because our heart is already at peace with the Divine.
This is how real love operates—not out of need, but out of overflow. It's about serving, not seeking to be served. It's about walking with someone, not clinging to them to feel whole. True love doesn't demand—it gives, carries, uplifts.
TRUE INTIMACY ISN'T JUST BASED ON NEEDS!
Real intimacy comes when both partners are walking their own paths of spiritual growth and are equally devoted to something greater than themselves. That's when love becomes more than just emotional comfort — it becomes a sacred partnership.
Popular self-help advice often centers around meeting each other's "love languages."
Love in its purest sense is not merely an emotion; it is a sacred offering of self in the service of the Lord. And maybe — just maybe — the order we crave in our fractured lives begins not by chasing human affection, but by anchoring ourselves in devoted surrender to the all-loving Kṛṣṇa, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, from whom all love eternally flows.
